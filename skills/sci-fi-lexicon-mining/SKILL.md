# Speculative Fiction Lexicon Archaeology

## Purpose
Mine unusual lexical patterns from a defined science-fiction era, subgenre or author cohort. Seek *formation mechanisms* and brief attested terms, not copycat names or recognizable fictional marks.

## When to use
Choose this when its mechanism yields a meaningfully different creative search direction from the branches explored so far. Avoid when the user has explicitly rejected the required sound, imagery or lexical source style.

## Inputs
Use the current brand brief: offer, target audience, desired character, exclusions and user taste. Infer provisional constraints when not provided. User can communicate in any language; names must be in English-compatible Latin script.

## Procedure
1. Propose contrasting eras/corpora: 1950s magazine SF, 1960s New Wave, 1970s ecological SF, contemporary solarpunk, early translated speculative fiction.
2. Ask for a direction or choose a provisional one explicitly. Define a source corpus of 5–12 identifiable works with bibliographic metadata and accessible reference material.
3. Research author bibliographies, available indexes, dictionaries of SF terms, book reviews, legal excerpts or accessible public-domain texts. Record precise source for any lexical finding.
4. Extract up to 20 short distinctive terms or patterns; tag each as actually attested or conjectural. Do not quote large passages or use signature franchise names as deliverables.
5. Analyze how words were made: foreign-root grafting, clipped technical compounds, pseudo-taxonomy, altered morphemes, phonotactics or semantic displacement.
6. Apply those *patterns* to the brand's concepts, generating 20–30 genuinely new candidates rather than simply copying fictional proper nouns.
7. Present clusters and the research trail. Offer choices: another decade, subgenre, linguistic operator, or different source universe.

## Research plan and provenance
Search work and author bibliographies, scholarly articles on SF neologisms, historical SF dictionaries, and accessible licensed text samples. Example queries: “1960s New Wave science fiction neologisms scholarly analysis”; “science fiction coined terms corpus study”. Cite each actual term; if a book cannot be accessed, never imply it was read.

## Interaction and navigation
Offer preview directions before conducting costly deep research. After generating a cluster, ask whether to (a) dig deeper into it, (b) explore another source territory, (c) combine this with another method, (d) return to the method menu or (e) shortlist selected candidates. The user decides when to stop.

## Output contract
For each shortlisted-for-discussion suggestion include: `candidate`, `creative tone`, `derivation`, `lexical status` (`verified attested`, `unverified attested`, `adapted`, `invented`), `source reference if research-derived`, and `spoken/pronunciation note when helpful`. Keep claims proportionate to source evidence.

## Example (synthetic; not research-verified)
Synthetic illustration: mimic a pseudo-scientific suffix pattern to create `Veloryn` (invented), rather than borrowing a famous fictional object or place.

## Failure modes
- Do not claim that a source, dictionary entry or etymology was checked without actually checking it.
- Do not label a word as original, legally available or trademark-safe.
- Avoid presenting dozens of orthographic variants as conceptually diverse work.
- Keep the user's rejected directions visible in the session log, and do not silently reintroduce them.
- For culturally located vocabulary, preserve nuance and flag likely sensitive material.
