# Six ways to reach a name

These compact examples show what the agent actually changes or discovers. All brand briefs, preferences and constructed names are illustrative. The historical lexical fact is supported by the linked evidence; none of these names is a cleared or recommended brand.

## 1. Combine two intact words

**Brief:** a welcoming space where people make things together.

**Method:** [intact-word compounding](../skills/intact-word-compounding/SKILL.md).

```text
open + bench → Open Bench
```

Both words survive unchanged. The relationship is legible: an accessible place to work. The tradeoff is that “bench” may suggest a physical workshop rather than a digital service.

**Next move:** keep the accessible tone but try other complete partner words. Cutting letters would start a different method.

## 2. Blend fragments from two words

**Brief:** a calm network for independent makers.

**Method:** [fragment blending](../skills/fragment-blending/SKILL.md).

```text
weave → keep weav, remove e
agora → keep ora, remove ag
weav + ora → Weavora
```

Both inputs lose material. The name is invented from supplied ingredients; no researched etymology or audience recognition is claimed. “Weave” remains easier to recover than “agora.”

**Next move:** vary the second fragment while keeping `weav`, then compare readability. An attractive join still needs a useful meaning.

## 3. Share an overlapping junction

**Brief:** warm, luminous tools for creative work.

**Method:** [overlap telescoping](../skills/overlap-telescoping/SKILL.md).

```text
gleam = gle + am
amber = am + ber
gle + am + ber → Gleamber
```

The shared `am` appears once. Both original words can be reconstructed through the join. A proposed reading is GLEEM-ber; the shared spelling does not preserve amber's original vowel sound.

**Next move:** find other genuinely matching endings/beginnings. Do not label an arbitrary cut-and-join an overlap.

## 4. Discover a word and keep it

**Brief:** an unhurried evening writing room.

**Method:** [historical lexical archaeology](../skills/historical-lexical-archaeology/SKILL.md).

```text
dated dictionary senses and variants → Gloaming
spelling transformation → none
```

The [recorded DSL entries](../docs/research/HISTORICAL_METHODS_EVIDENCE.md) support the twilight sense and regional/historical forms. This is an attested word, not an invention. Old witnesses do not prove the word is obsolete or globally rare. A “gloom” association may work against a warm brief.

**Next move:** compare other supported evening words, or retain Gloaming unchanged. Adaptation is optional.

## 5. Construct a sound with no invented origin story

**Brief:** a precise but welcoming abstract product name.

**Method:** [phonetic invention](../skills/phonetic-invention/SKILL.md).

```text
t + ɛ ; l + oʊ → /ˈtɛ.loʊ/ → Telo
```

Two open syllables, initial stress, no semantic roots. Telo is an invented form in this exercise; the pronunciation is a target. It may need more explanation than a familiar English compound.

**Next move:** change one sound slot at a time. Do not invent a foreign or ancient meaning to make the result sound more substantial.

## 6. Change the writing, keep the target sound

**Brief:** retain the spoken word “cove,” explore another written identity.

**Method:** [respelling](../skills/respelling/SKILL.md).

```text
cove /koʊv/ → c changes to k → Kove /koʊv/
```

One grapheme changes; the intended sound remains fixed. Kove is an adapted proposal from a supplied anchor, with an untested reading target. The new spelling adds a learning burden and establishes no availability or legal advantage.

**Next move:** compare the original and variant in the same sentence. If the sound changes intentionally, record a phonetic operation instead.

## What the user sees

An ordinary first response can stay small:

> For a kitchen-tool repair service, I would compare **Second Stir**, **Ready Handle** and **Good Keep**. Second Stir gives a concrete kitchen image, but may sound spoon-specific. Ready Handle feels practical, but might imply speed. Good Keep suggests care, but says less about repair. I would start with Second Stir. These are illustrative constructed names, with no researched origin claims.
