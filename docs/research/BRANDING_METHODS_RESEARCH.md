# Applied brand-naming methods for research-enabled agents

Research date: 2026-11-10. Status: research and editorial recommendations, not accepted skills.

## Scope and evidence

This review prioritizes techniques actually discussed in brand-naming practice and amenable to explicit agent operations. It does not establish a numerical target, prove commercial effectiveness, or perform clearance. The owner prefers narrow techniques with detailed instructions even when several belong to one family.

Sources below were opened, not merely discovered through snippets. Specific consulted sections are recorded in the bibliography. Pages containing unrelated legal or domain guidance were used only for creative research. Published categories of names are not automatically reproducible methods. A professional's recommendation is evidence of practice, not experimental validation.

**Attribution convention:** “Source basis” describes what the publication supports. “Agent adaptation” is this project's proposed implementation, including artifact formats, branch choices, and recovery rules. These implementation details must not be represented as the source author's methodology. The suggested slugs below are editorial proposals, not existing skill files.

## Main findings

1. The current catalog overrepresents exotic source domains and underrepresents ordinary but powerful brand-naming operations: phrase extraction, double meaning, rhyme, onomatopoeia, visual association, and systematic compound construction.
2. Research and generation should remain separable but connected. A category-language map or a project vocabulary is useful without being an independent name-construction algorithm.
3. Narrow skills can overlap without being duplicates. Telescoping, fragment blending, and intact-word compounds have different join rules. Rhyme, alliteration, repetition, and phonetic attribute contrast impose different constraints.
4. Agent suitability comes from inspectable intermediate artifacts: source-word ledgers, join maps, phrase tables, sound contrasts, and branch records. It does not follow merely from adding “generate 30 names” to a category description.
5. Research-dependent claims need evidence; aesthetic predictions need feedback. An agent can propose sound or association hypotheses, but cannot simulate actual consumer research and report it as observation.

## Recommended generation techniques

These are candidates for separate detailed skills. None is ready by virtue of this report. Sources are linked through IDs in the bibliography.

### G01. Overlap telescoping — `overlap-telescoping`

**Source basis:** S04 explicitly distinguishes overlapping words from combining fragments.

**Agent adaptation:** Input two meaningful word banks. Find a shared letter or sound sequence at a possible junction; construct forms preserving that overlap once. Keep a join map showing both originals and the shared segment. Check spoken continuity separately from spelling. Ask which semantic pairing deserves deeper search.

**Boundary:** Unlike fragment blending, a genuine shared sequence is required. Unlike compounding, material is merged at the join. If no useful overlap exists, change the partner bank rather than disguise a compound as telescoping.

### G02. Fragment blending — `fragment-blending`

**Source basis:** S04 describes making names from portions of two words; S05 provides a practical search route for compatible joins.

**Agent adaptation:** Start with two concepts, mark meaningful or pronounceable cuts, and pair retained heads and tails. Record deleted material. Present alternatives that differ in semantic balance and join smoothness. The user chooses which meaning should remain recognizable.

**Boundary:** Overlap is optional. This is different from syllable splicing when the fragments must preserve interpretable links to the two source concepts.

### G03. Intact-word compounding — `intact-word-compounding`

**Source basis:** S05 discusses whole-word combinations and the value of meaningful ingredients.

**Agent adaptation:** Build separate banks for an identity anchor and an unexpected modifier or object. Keep words intact; test both orders and open/closed forms. Record the relationship each pair implies. Show plausible versus deliberately surprising combinations and ask how much surprise feels right.

**Boundary:** No clipping or fusion. Contrast is one pairing strategy, not a requirement for every compound.

### G04. Prefix attachment — `prefix-attachment`

**Source basis:** S01 discusses adding prefixes; S04 proposes beginning with meaningful word parts.

**Agent adaptation:** Hold a stem bank constant and vary only the prefix operator: direction, repetition, relation, scale, or invented signal. Produce a stem-by-prefix matrix and explain the shift each creates. Ask the user to choose the intended stance. Reject additions that contribute only a familiar startup costume.

**Boundary:** Distinct from suffix attachment because the initial segment changes the name's entry sound and interpretive framing. Historical root analysis is a different research operation.

### G05. Suffix attachment — `suffix-attachment`

**Source basis:** S01 discusses suffixes and word endings.

**Agent adaptation:** Keep semantic stems fixed, then explore endings that suggest an action, person, place, instrument, or invented cadence. Record which endings are meaningful versus purely invented. Compare forms with different stress patterns. Ask which perceived role and tone suit the brand.

**Boundary:** Unlike clipping, this adds material. Unlike free phonetic invention, the source stem remains an explicit input.

### G06. Clipping — `clipping`

**Source basis:** S01 includes truncation; S05 discusses shortening existing words.

**Agent adaptation:** Select long evocative source words and mark several possible cut points. Remove material without adding a new root. Compare recognizability, pronunciation ambiguity, and the surviving image. Present short forms beside originals so the user can choose whether recognition or abstraction matters more.

**Boundary:** Cutting is the operator; adding endings belongs to a later hybrid step. Do not call arbitrary internal strings meaningful roots.

### G07. Pronunciation-preserving respelling — `respelling`

**Source basis:** S05 discusses spelling changes; S12 includes playful misspellings in brand wordplay.

**Agent adaptation:** Start with a spoken word or candidate. Change one orthographic feature while aiming to preserve its intended pronunciation. Keep an original/variant/pronunciation-risk table. Ask whether the visual personality compensates for spelling friction. Do not claim a human spell-back test occurred.

**Boundary:** A spelling operator, not a sound mutation. If pronunciation changes intentionally, label it a different operation.

### G08. Rhyme pairing — `rhyme-pairing`

**Source basis:** S04 recommends rhyming tools; S09 discusses rhyming brand names.

**Agent adaptation:** Choose a semantic anchor and find full and near-rhyme partners. Compare stress and meaning, not spelling endings alone. Separate playful, crisp, and awkward results. Ask the user whether the mnemonic playfulness fits the intended brand.

**Boundary:** Repeats a stressed vowel and following sounds; alliteration repeats onsets. Nursery-rhyme tone is a recoverable mismatch, not an automatic benefit.

### G09. Alliterative pairing — `alliterative-pairing`

**Source basis:** S04 and S12 identify alliteration as brand wordplay.

**Agent adaptation:** Build two semantic role banks and constrain initial sounds, including differently spelled words with matching onsets. Preserve varied meanings and lengths. Record which onset is repeated. Let the user compare restrained and conspicuous repetitions.

**Boundary:** Do not substitute same-letter spelling for sound analysis. Different from rhyme and full repetition; a compound can use any of these as a second constraint.

### G10. Reduplication and echo forms — `reduplication`

**Source basis:** S10 discusses repeated sound structures in confectionery names; S05 includes word doubling.

**Agent adaptation:** Begin with a useful syllable or short word. Produce exact repetition, changed-vowel echoes, and changed-onset echoes as separately labeled modes. Compare playfulness against maturity. Show only a small set before the user chooses an acceptable degree of repetition.

**Boundary:** Reuses a whole unit with controlled variation; accidental alliteration is insufficient. Avoid treating the source's broad consumer claims as guaranteed outcomes.

### G11. Sound-symbolic attribute contrast — `sound-symbolism`

**Source basis:** S11 experimentally examines phonetic contrasts in fictitious brand names; S10 provides practitioner applications.

**Agent adaptation:** Choose one relevant attribute and generate matched forms differing in one sound feature. Document the hypothesized contrast and other uncontrolled differences. Ask the user for actual impressions before expanding a family.

**Boundary:** An attribute-driven hypothesis, unlike general pleasant-sounding invention. S11's context-specific findings do not justify a universal table of letter personalities. Work with phonemes, not assumed meanings of alphabet letters.

### G12. Onomatopoeic naming — `onomatopoeic-naming`

**Source basis:** S09 identifies brand names drawing on product-use sounds.

**Agent adaptation:** Describe or research the actual sounds of use, activation, movement, or completion. Translate a sound event into several English-compatible spellings. Record whether the sound was observed, reported, or imagined. Ask whether literal imitation or subtle suggestion is preferable.

**Boundary:** Starts from an event's audible character. General sound symbolism need not imitate anything; do not invent acoustic product facts.

### G13. Idiom and phrase extraction — `phrase-extraction`

**Source basis:** S07 recommends attending to everyday phrases and idioms; S09 discusses names derived from familiar expressions.

**Agent adaptation:** Search expressions associated with the brand's promise. Verify meaning and register. Extract a compact, unchanged fragment that retains useful associations. Keep full expression, fragment, source, and lost context. Ask which narrative resonates without requiring the audience to know the origin.

**Boundary:** Extracts or recontextualizes language without replacing it. Phrase substitution is a different operator.

### G14. Phrase substitution and phonetic punning — `phrase-substitution`

**Source basis:** S04 discusses puns; S12 includes them in brand wordplay. This narrow operator is an editorial specialization of that broader category.

**Agent adaptation:** Collect recognizable host phrases, identify a replaceable slot, and insert a relevant near-sounding word. Record the host, substitution, retained sound cue, and new meaning. Ask the user to judge whether the joke supports the brand or overwhelms it.

**Boundary:** Changes a known expression. Unlike blending, the familiar phrase is essential to the effect; unlike extraction, it rewrites rather than shortens.

### G15. Double-meaning intersection — `double-meaning`

**Source basis:** S04 explicitly describes using multiple applicable meanings of a word or phrase.

**Agent adaptation:** Build a table of words with at least two independently supportable readings: one product-related, one emotional, social, or conceptual. Verify both senses and test whether each is accessible without explanation. Let the user choose which reading should dominate.

**Boundary:** Preserves one form carrying multiple readings. A phonetic pun may depend on two different forms that sound alike; keep those derivations separate.

### G16. Productive opposition — `productive-opposition`

**Source basis:** S04 discusses incongruous pairings; S09 includes oxymoronic brand names.

**Agent adaptation:** Select a real positioning tension, such as power with gentleness. Generate terms from each pole, pair them, and state a plausible reconciliation. Ask whether the tension feels intriguing or contradictory to the offer.

**Boundary:** Semantic opposition is required. Two distant nouns are not necessarily a paradox. Recover by grounding the tension in an actual product truth.

### G17. Metaphorical positioning transfer — `metaphor-mining`

**Source basis:** S03 explains metaphorical naming tied to positioning rather than literal description.

**Agent adaptation:** Choose one brand promise; propose source worlds embodying it. Research source objects and processes, then map their relevant properties to the promise. Keep both useful and misleading implications. The user selects the world and depth.

**Boundary:** Metaphor is the operator; astronomy, folklore, and wine are universes. Do not create separate methods merely by changing them.

### G18. Benefit-level renaming — `benefit-level-renaming`

**Source basis:** S13 presents a benefit ladder; S08 connects naming directions with functional and emotional positioning.

**Agent adaptation:** Rewrite one feature at successive consequence levels, ending at a believable user experience or identity. Generate names at each level and show the path. Ask which level the brand can credibly promise.

**Boundary:** Moves upward within a benefit chain, whereas metaphor moves into another domain. Treat this as provisional pending stronger independent practitioner corroboration for the narrow procedure.

### G19. Directive naming — `directive-naming`

**Source basis:** S09 identifies names using commands or invitations.

**Agent adaptation:** Convert the desired customer action into varied imperative constructions, then generate gentler invitations and more assertive versions. Record implied speaker, listener, and action. Ask whether the voice feels empowering or pushy.

**Boundary:** A grammatical speech-act operator, not any name containing a verb. It warrants its own skill only with demonstrations of compact brand names rather than slogans alone.

### G20. Cross-language concept search — `cross-language-mining`

**Source basis:** S01 includes translation-based exploration; S16 identifies useful contextual language resources.

**Agent adaptation:** Start with a concept cluster and selected languages. Consult actual dictionary entries, preserve original scripts and semantic range, then propose direct forms and traceable English adaptations. Ask which sound and meaning direction to deepen. Machine translation supplies leads, not verification.

**Boundary:** Translates/searches concepts across languages. Etymological tracing follows documented historical relationships instead. Do not infer cultural character from a language family.

### G21. Visual-to-verbal extraction — `visual-to-verbal`

**Source basis:** S01 suggests evocative image collections for name ideation; S12 recommends moodboards in brand clarification.

**Agent adaptation:** Use accessible reference images or an explicitly hypothetical visual description. Extract shapes, gestures, textures, spatial relationships, and actions before generating words. Preserve image-to-observation-to-name links. The user picks a visual character, not merely a favorite picture.

**Boundary:** Begins with nonverbal material. If no images can actually be inspected, disclose that limitation; do not invent visual observations. Further transformation can use other skills.

## Supporting skills: useful, but not additional name-generation counts

### S-A. Category-language mapping

**Source basis:** S02 maps competitor names to reveal repeated conventions and alternative creative territory.

**Agent adaptation:** Collect a bounded, source-backed sample from company/product sites. Classify name structure, sounds, semantic fields, and tone. Propose several departures from observed patterns and let the user choose how far to depart. Missing patterns in a small sample are not proof of market-wide absence.

**Artifact:** Dated comparison table plus creative hypotheses. This is not a conflict search and must not become legal screening.

### S-B. Project-vocabulary expansion

**Source basis:** S01 organizes vocabulary around brief messages; S06 demonstrates role-based vocabulary combination.

**Agent adaptation:** Turn the brief into distinct semantic roles, expand each with verified related words, then retain definitions and reasons for inclusion. The user chooses promising meaning clusters. A noun/process matrix can feed compounding or blending without being counted as another spelling operator.

**Artifact:** Tagged ingredient banks. Reject thesaurus drift that erases the original promise.

### S-C. Association-chain exploration

**Source basis:** S05 describes expanding ideas through related words and diverse references.

**Agent adaptation:** Use a bounded graph where each hop states its relationship and source status. Show several paths at different semantic distances. The user chooses a branch; preserve rejected branches. Stop when the relationship requires a contrived explanation.

**Artifact:** Traceable paths. It can yield real-word names directly, but often supplies ingredients for other techniques.

### S-D. Contextual comparison and feedback

**Source basis:** S14 structures agent naming directions and shortlist work; S13 includes context testing. These are workflow examples, not evidence of efficacy.

**Agent adaptation:** Place competing candidates in identical plain-text introductions, packaging descriptors, or product-family scenarios. Ask the user for impressions and note observed feedback separately from agent predictions. Do not silently place candidates on the saved shortlist.

**Artifact:** Comparable contexts and feedback log. Real audience research requires real participants; role-play does not substitute for it.

### S-E. Family-rule continuation

**Source basis:** S01 and S09 discuss portfolio naming architecture.

**Agent adaptation:** Infer a tentative naming rule from supplied existing names, state uncertainty, and ask which elements must persist. Generate a new sibling and hypothetical future siblings to expose brittle rules.

**Artifact:** Explicit family grammar. This constrains multiple generation methods; it is not simply another lexical universe.

## Catalog recommendations

### Add or clarify first

- Add phrase extraction, phrase substitution, double meaning, rhyme, onomatopoeia, directive naming, and visual-to-verbal extraction.
- Split broad blending into explicit overlap and non-overlap procedures if full examples show useful differences.
- Split prefix and suffix attachment into detailed narrow skills; keep an umbrella navigation page.
- Preserve narrow phonetic techniques. The owner explicitly prefers individual treatment; do not merge them merely because they share a family.
- Keep support skills in a visible, separately labeled group. They are useful agent capabilities even when they do not produce a name alone.

### Retain but do not overstate current evidence

The present review does not establish strong branding-specific evidence for consonant-skeleton generation, vowel harmony, back-formation, anagram generation, cross-taxonomy collision, or object biography. They may be valuable editorial techniques; label them as such until independently sourced and demonstrated. Likewise, an agent procedure may be original while its linguistic operation is established.

Historical vocabulary, etymology, rare lexical research, and literary word-formation analysis remain important. They need a dedicated research pass; this report's stronger coverage of mainstream practice should not remove the project's exploratory character.

### Move domain-only entries into universes

Weather, minerals, marine biology, wine/perfume, stagecraft, and instrument-making are rich source worlds. For each, record which operators it enables: definition-based metaphor, rare-word extraction, structural analogy, pattern imitation, or morphological transformation. Preserve any genuinely different research protocol as a reusable technique, not a method named solely after the field.

### Suggested first development batch

Use a deliberately contrasting batch rather than the largest numerical group:

1. Overlap telescoping: precise construction and inspectable joins.
2. Double meaning: semantic intersection without word alteration.
3. Phrase extraction: research and cultural register.
4. Sound-symbolic attribute contrast: controlled hypotheses and user feedback.
5. Metaphorical positioning transfer: agent-proposed universes and deeper research.
6. Visual-to-verbal extraction: an alternative starting modality with explicit capability fallback.

For every skill, demonstrate a full small run, a weak result and recovery, a user-led branch change, and a source ledger. This is a proposed authoring order, not an approved catalog or new readiness claim.

## Addendum: MECE review and missing operators

Added after the owner's clarification. MECE applies to the classification of the **primary operation in a documented step**, not to finished names. A name can be produced by several operations in sequence. Give each operation one primary home; keep universe, source origin, sound constraints, output form, and brand tone as cross-cutting tags. Avoid asserting that the present list is collectively exhaustive.

A practical primary-home test asks what changed in the step:

| Primary operation | What the step changes or chooses | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Source discovery | Where and how material is found | Dictionary expansion, historical tracing, source-story extraction |
| Semantic selection/transfer | Which meaning, relationship, or promise becomes name material | Metaphor, metonymy, double meaning, benefit shift |
| Structural word construction | How lexical units are assembled or reduced | Compounding, clipping, affixation, initial-letter compression |
| Sound construction | How spoken form is invented or constrained | Phonetic invention, syllable recombination, rhyme, repetition |
| Orthographic transformation | How a spoken/lexical form is written | Respelling |
| Evaluation/navigation | How evidence and preferences guide the next branch | Category mapping, contextual comparison, family-rule checks |

This is an editorial routing rule, not a claim that language has perfectly disjoint natural categories. For example, alliteration can constrain a compound; the compound construction step has structural home and an alliteration tag, while an alliteration-first generation step has sound home. Record both steps rather than count a hybrid as a newly discovered method.

### Missing operator: metonymic selection — `metonymic-selection`

**Source basis:** S17 defines metonymy as substitution through association. This verifies the linguistic distinction, not a complete agency procedure.

**Agent adaptation:** Map a product activity into participants, instruments, places, materials, traces, and characteristic gestures. Select a salient associated element to represent the whole. Preserve the actual relationship and ask which element users would recognize.

**Boundary:** Contiguity within a relevant situation rather than resemblance across domains. An unrelated animal standing for courage is metaphor; an actual tool standing for its craft is metonymy. Source-domain tags alone do not establish either mechanism.

### Missing operator: acronym construction — `acronym-construction`

**Source basis:** S17 distinguishes a word-pronounced acronym from a letter-pronounced initialism. S18 discusses branding tradeoffs of abbreviated names.

**Agent adaptation:** Begin with meaningful, independently acceptable phrase alternatives. Extract their initial letters, compare pronounceable sequences, and preserve each expansion. Ask whether the abbreviated form retains enough identity without explaining the expansion.

**Boundary:** Letter extraction from an actual phrase; not blending and not inventing a word followed by a fabricated expansion. Reverse expansion/backronym generation would need a separate operator and explicit label.

### Missing operator: initialism construction — `initialism-construction`

**Source basis:** S17 distinguishes letter-by-letter pronunciation; S18 provides practitioner discussion and an agency-reported abbreviation case.

**Agent adaptation:** Compress meaningful source phrases into initials, evaluate spoken letter sequences and ambiguity, and compare them in ordinary introductions. Record whether the expansion is pre-existing or newly proposed. User choice concerns continuity versus emotional expressiveness.

**Boundary:** Letter-name sequence rather than word pronunciation. Include this skill without assuming it is desirable for every new brand or that brevity ensures recall.

### Missing discovery operator: personal, place, and story-origin extraction — `origin-extraction`

**Source basis:** S12 discusses founder/place names and examples of personal-name adaptation; S17 defines eponyms. Those are origin categories, not methods by themselves.

**Agent adaptation:** Ask for authentic material already available: people, places, milestones, artifacts, phrases, and formative events. Extract compact name-bearing tokens, record the real connection, and compare direct use with separately labeled clipping or blending. User participation is essential when the facts are private or personally meaningful.

**Boundary:** Discovery from a true brand-specific history. Do not invent a provenance story. Person, place, and event remain source tags unless their retrieval procedures materially differ. Existing-world references used solely for imagery belong to metaphor instead.

### Narrow sound operator to retain: syllable recombination — `syllable-recombination`

**Source basis:** S17 confirms the broad existence of constructed and invented forms, but does not establish this narrow procedure. Branding-specific operational evidence remains incomplete.

**Agent adaptation:** Build a bank of syllables with explicit pronunciation and stress possibilities. Recombine them without requiring source-word meanings; compare boundaries and consonant clusters. Ask the user to select a sound family. Any accidental source resemblance remains unverified.

**Boundary:** Begins with already formed syllable units, unlike phonetic invention from onset/nucleus/coda constraints. Unlike fragment blending, semantic lineage to source words is not required. Preserve as its own experimental skill rather than collapse into blending.

### Narrow sound operator to retain: phonetic invention — `phonetic-invention`

**Source basis:** S19 reports creation programs generating names without formal meanings; S20 describes sound-direction and linguistic-element construction. Neither exposes a complete reproducible proprietary algorithm.

**Agent adaptation:** Define permitted onsets, vowels, codas, stress, and syllable count; construct several contrasting sound palettes and generate forms within each. Record the constraints, show pronunciation uncertainty, and get human preferences before refinement.

**Boundary:** Constructs spoken form without source roots. Sound symbolism supplies optional attribute hypotheses, syllable recombination supplies pre-built chunks, and spelling design follows rather than defines the operation.

### MECE consequences for the starter catalog

- Keep compounding, fragment blending, clipping, prefixation, suffixation, syllable recombination, and phonetic invention separately documented. Shared membership in word creation does not make them duplicates.
- Keep double meaning distinct from metonymy, metaphor, phrase extraction, and phrase substitution. Record the actual relation used, not merely “evocative.”
- Add acronym and initialism operations where relevant, while retaining honest limitations of letter strings as new-brand vehicles.
- Treat founder/place/story origins as research inputs unless a specific extraction or transformation procedure is shown.
- Keep the count open. Audit coverage against operator families and missing creative routes; do not use MECE to force artificial equal-sized groups or delete useful narrow procedures.
